**Abstract**

This paper critically reviews Agbola and Acupan’s (2010) study of
international labour migration in the Philippines. The study asks how
economic, demographic, and political factors affect emigration from the
Philippines. It uses yearly national data from 1975 to 2005, which gives
only 31 observations, and an error correction model (ECM). The study
finds four statistically significant push factors. Unemployment raises
emigration, while adult literacy, political instability, and population
density lower it. Cost of migration, per capita income, inflation, and
income inequality are not statistically significant. This review finds
that the study asks an important question and follows careful
time-series steps. However, its results should be read with care for
four reasons: (a) the sample is small, (b) two key measures are weak
proxies, (c) pull factors from destination countries are missing, and
(d) the explanation for the adult literacy result conflicts with
evidence the authors themselves cite. The review ends with four
directions for further research: splitting the data by type and skill of
migrant, adding pull factors, updating the data and testing for
structural breaks, and using household survey data.

*Keywords:* emigration, push factors, error correction model, overseas
Filipino workers, critical review

**Push Factors at Home and Filipino Emigration: A Critical Review of
Agbola and Acupan (2010)**

Many Filipinos leave the country to live or work abroad. About 8.2
million Filipinos live or work in more than 193 countries, and together
they form the third-largest diaspora in the world (Agbola & Acupan,
2010). In 2007, they sent home US\$14.4 billion in remittances, which is
money sent home by workers abroad (Agbola & Acupan, 2010). Because
emigration is this large, it is important to know what causes it.

Agbola and Acupan (2010) study this question in *Economic Systems*. They
use yearly national data from 1975 to 2005 and an error correction model
(ECM) to test which conditions in the Philippines push Filipinos to
leave. In short, the study asks which push factors in the Philippines
lead Filipinos to emigrate.

This paper gives a critical review of that study. It covers six parts:
(a) the research problem and gap, (b) the theoretical framework, (c) the
data, (d) the methodology, (e) the key findings, and (f) the limitations
and areas for further research. It also gives a critical assessment of
the study’s strengths and weaknesses. To keep the review clear, each
technical term keeps the same meaning throughout the paper. Table 1
defines these terms.

**Table 1**

# Research Problem and Gap

The main message of the research problem is that emigration became
permanent, but its causes were not well established. The Philippines
began sending workers abroad in the early 1970s. At first, this was a
short-term fix for high unemployment and a balance-of-payments crisis,
which happens when a country spends more foreign money than it earns
(Agbola & Acupan, 2010). Over time, emigration became a permanent part
of the economy.

The authors also show that the law on emigration is unclear. Republic
Act No. 8042, the Migrant Workers and Overseas Filipinos Act of 1995,
states that the State “does not promote overseas employment as a means
to sustain economic growth and achieve national development” (Section
2c, as cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010, p. 388). Yet the same law states
that the deployment of overseas Filipino workers “shall be encouraged”
(Section 2i, as cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010, p. 388).

The knowledge gap is that the causes of emigration from the Philippines
“are not well established” (Agbola & Acupan, 2010, p. 386). Policymakers
need evidence on which push factors matter most. The research question
is therefore: How do economic, demographic, and political factors affect
emigration from the Philippines?

The problem is clear and important. However, the authors do not review
earlier studies on Philippine emigration in detail. As a result, the gap
is stated more than it is shown.

# Theoretical Framework

The authors combine four theories into one model of push factors,
because no single theory explains emigration (Agbola & Acupan, 2010).
The first idea comes from Ravenstein (1885) and Todaro (1969): people
migrate when they expect a better life abroad (as cited in Agbola &
Acupan, 2010). The second is human capital theory (Hicks, 1939;
Sjaastad, 1962, as cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010). It treats migration
as an investment, like going to school, so more educated people are
expected to migrate more. The third is the Roy effect (Borjas, 1987, as
cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010), which says that income inequality
decides which skill groups leave. The fourth is the gravity model
(Greenwood, 1975; Adams & Page, 2003, as cited in Agbola & Acupan,
2010), which says that migration depends on income, population, and the
cost of migration.

From these ideas, the authors build one equation. In words, the
migration share depends on nine explanatory variables: cost of
migration, adult literacy, income inequality, income inequality squared,
political instability, inflation, population density, unemployment, and
per capita income.

The framework is a mix of theories, not one model. The theories are used
mainly to predict whether each coefficient should be positive or
negative. In addition, the gravity model normally compares two places,
the home country and the destination country. This study uses only push
factors from the Philippines, so pull factors from destination countries
are left out.

# Data

The study uses yearly national data from 1975 to 2005. This gives only
31 observations, one for each year. All of the data are secondary data,
which means other agencies collected them. Table 2 shows each variable,
how it is measured, where it comes from, and the sign the authors
expected for its coefficient (Agbola & Acupan, 2010).

**Table 2**

|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|---------------|
| Migration share                       | Migrants (temporary and permanent) ÷ population                | CFO; NSCB              | Explained     |
| Cost of migration                     | Average one-way airfare, Manila to top 24 destinations (proxy) | Authors’ estimate      | Negative      |
| Adult literacy                        | Adult literacy rate (proxy for education)                      | BSP; NSCB              | Positive      |
| Income inequality, income inequality² | Gini coefficient and its square (Roy effect)                   | Not stated             | Unclear       |
| Political instability                 | Number of leadership changes, coups, and uprisings             | Gastil (1987) approach | Negative      |

Statistical Coordination Board; BSP = Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas; IMF =
International Monetary Fund. Adapted from Agbola and Acupan (2010).

Two measures are proxies. Airfare stands in for the cost of migration,
and adult literacy stands in for education, because data on schooling
levels were not available for the whole period.

Two problems stand out. First, the paper does not state the source of
the income inequality data. This matters because the Philippines
measures the Gini coefficient only every few years, so the yearly values
were probably estimated, but the paper does not explain how. Second, the
migration share mixes temporary and permanent migrants, who may leave
for different reasons.

# Methodology

The study uses a standard five-step time-series method, and the authors
apply it carefully. It is a quantitative analysis of one country using
national data (Agbola & Acupan, 2010).

In Step 1, unit root tests, the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) and
Phillips–Perron (P–P) tests check whether each time series is
stationary. Most variables are not stationary at first, but they become
stationary after first differences. In Step 2, cointegration tests, the
Engle–Granger, Johansen, and Pesaran bounds tests all show that the
variables move together in the long run. In Step 3, choosing the model,
the authors use a general-to-specific approach: they start with a large
model and drop the terms that are not statistically significant, one at
a time. In Step 4, estimation, the ECM is estimated by ordinary least
squares (OLS) in the EViews 6.0 software. In Step 5, diagnostic tests,
the authors check the model for common problems, such as errors that are
linked over time, a wrong model form, and coefficients that are not
stable. The model passes all of these tests.

The steps are standard, but three problems remain. First, the Johansen
test finds five to eight long-run relationships, yet the ECM keeps only
one ECT. Second, the P–P test shows that per capita income is still not
stationary after first differences (t = −2.123), and the authors do not
discuss this. Third, 31 observations is a small sample for an ECM with
10 explanatory terms (nine variables plus the ECT). With a small sample,
the tests have low power, which means they may miss real effects.

## Model Fit

The adjusted R² of 0.63 means the model explains 63% of changes in the
migration share. The ECT of −0.776 means that about 78% of any gap from
the long-run path closes within one year. The F-statistic of 5.848 (p =
0.000) shows that the explanatory variables matter when taken together.

## Four Significant Push Factors

In short, unemployment raises emigration, and three other push factors
lower it. First, when unemployment goes up, emigration goes up
(coefficient = +0.236). Emigration acts as a “safety valve” when there
are not enough jobs at home. Second, when adult literacy goes up,
emigration goes down (coefficient = −0.659). This is the opposite of
what human capital theory predicts. The authors explain that most
Filipino migrants do low-skilled jobs abroad, so more education keeps
people at home (Agbola & Acupan, 2010).

Third, when political instability goes up, emigration goes down
(coefficient = −0.482). The migration share fell in unstable years such
as 1983, 1986, 2001, and 2005 (Agbola & Acupan, 2010). Fourth, when
population density goes up, emigration goes down (coefficient = −0.254).
This is the opposite of what was expected. The authors cannot fully
explain it, but they suggest that a larger population may create more
demand for local goods and therefore more jobs at home (Agbola & Acupan,
2010).

## Results That Are Not Statistically Significant

Income, inflation, cost of migration, and income inequality do not
significantly affect emigration. Cost of migration, per capita income,
and inflation have the expected negative sign, but their effects could
be due to chance. Income inequality and income inequality squared are
also not statistically significant, so the Roy effect does not hold for
the Philippines.

## The Authors’ Conclusions

The authors conclude that the main push factors are unemployment, adult
literacy, political instability, and population density. They recommend
that the government (a) collect and track migration data better, (b)
sign agreements with destination countries to protect overseas Filipino
workers, and (c) build a home economy with enough jobs so that the
country relies less on emigration (Agbola & Acupan, 2010).

# Critical Assessment

Overall, the study uses careful methods, but it has a small sample, weak
proxies, and missing pull factors.

## Strengths

The study has four main strengths. First, it asks an important question
for a country that sends many workers abroad. Second, it follows careful
steps: unit root tests, then cointegration tests, then the ECM. Third,
it reports all of its diagnostic tests, and the model passes them.
Fourth, it gives a clear policy background, including the unclear
wording of Republic Act No. 8042.

## Weaknesses

The study also has five weaknesses. The first is the small sample.
Thirty-one yearly observations is small for 10 explanatory terms, so the
results may not be reliable.

The second is the use of weak proxies. Airfare shows only the minimum
cost of migration, because it leaves out job placement fees and other
costs. Adult literacy in the Philippines was already very high for most
of the study period, so it changed little and is a weak proxy for
education.

The third is that the study uses push factors only. It leaves out pull
factors from destination countries, such as job demand in the Middle
East, which drove much of Filipino labour emigration from the 1970s
onward.

The fourth is a conflicting explanation. The authors say that adult
literacy lowers emigration because most migrants are low-skilled.
However, the paper itself cites Alburo and Abella (2002), who found that
more Filipino professionals went abroad than were added to the workforce
at home (as cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010). This evidence does not fit
the “low-skilled migrants” explanation.

The fifth is unclear test results. Per capita income is not stationary
in the P–P test, even after first differences, and the authors do not
test for structural breaks, which are sudden changes in the data.
Examples of possible breaks are the 1986 EDSA Revolution and the 1997
Asian financial crisis.

# Limitations and Areas for Further Research

Better data and pull factors are the next steps for research on Filipino
emigration.

## Limitations Identified by the Authors

The authors name four limitations. Migration data are hard to collect
and compile. Airfare shows only the minimum cost of migration. The
population density result is hard to explain and needs more research.
Finally, the data cannot prove the political instability story (Agbola &
Acupan, 2010).

## Directions for Future Research

Building on the weaknesses above, this review suggests four directions.
First, future studies should split the data into temporary and permanent
migrants, and group migrants by skill level. Second, they should add
pull factors, such as wages and job demand in destination countries.
Third, they should update the data past 2005 and test for structural
breaks, such as the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2008 global
financial crisis, and COVID-19. Fourth, they should use household survey
data on individual migrants to test human capital theory directly.

# Conclusion

Push factors at home shape Filipino emigration. Agbola and Acupan (2010)
show that unemployment raises emigration, while adult literacy,
political instability, and population density lower it. The study gives
useful early evidence and a clear policy message: build a home economy
with enough jobs. However, the small sample, the weak proxies, and the
missing pull factors mean that the results should be read with care.
They are a starting point for further research, not a final answer.
