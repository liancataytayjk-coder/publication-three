**Push Factors and Filipino Emigration: A Critical Review of Agbola and
Acupan (2010)**

Cataytay · University of Southeastern Philippines

The Philippines exports labour on a scale few economies match: about 8.2
million Filipinos live or work abroad, and their remittances reached
US\$14.4 billion in 2007 (Agbola & Acupan, 2010). Labour export began in
the 1970s as a short-term fix for unemployment and a balance-of-payments
crisis but became permanent, while policy stays ambiguous: Republic Act
No. 8042 says the State does not promote overseas work, yet encourages
deployment. Agbola and Acupan (2010) address a clear gap, since the
causes of emigration from the Philippines “are not well established” (p.
386). They ask how economic, demographic, and political factors affect
emigration.

The framework blends several theories. Expected-income models (Todaro,
1969) predict that people migrate when the expected gain abroad exceeds
the cost; human capital theory treats migration as an investment, so
education should raise emigration; the Roy effect links income
inequality to which skill groups leave; and the gravity model ties
migration to income, population, and the cost of migration (as cited in
Agbola & Acupan, 2010). Together they yield one push-factor equation for
the migration share (migrants as a share of the population) based only
on domestic explanatory variables. The study uses yearly national data
for 1975–2005. Because the series are not stationary in levels, the
authors confirm a long-run cointegrating relationship and estimate an
error correction model (ECM), which separates short-run changes from the
adjustment back to long-run equilibrium.

The results tell a push story. Unemployment raises emigration, as
expected if emigration is a safety valve for surplus labour. Adult
literacy, political instability, and population density lower it. The
literacy result contradicts human capital theory, and the authors
attribute it to the low-skilled profile of Filipino migrants. Per capita
income, inflation, migration cost, and income inequality are not
statistically significant, so the Roy effect does not hold. The model
explains 63% of changes in the migration share, and the error correction
term (−0.776) implies that about 78% of any deviation from equilibrium
is corrected within a year. The policy lesson is to build a domestic
economy that absorbs labour rather than exports it.

The study is policy-relevant and its time-series steps are careful, but
four weaknesses stand out. Thirty-one observations are thin for ten
explanatory terms, so the tests have low power. Airfare and adult
literacy are weak proxies for migration cost and education. Most
importantly, a push-only model ignores pull factors, above all labour
demand in destination countries such as the Gulf states, which drove
much of Filipino emigration. The low-skill explanation also clashes with
evidence the authors themselves cite of large professional outflows
(Alburo & Abella, 2002, as cited in Agbola & Acupan, 2010).

Future research should separate temporary from permanent and skilled
from low-skilled migrants, add destination-side pull factors in a
bilateral framework, extend the data past 2005 with tests for structural
breaks, and use household survey data to test human capital theory
directly. Agbola and Acupan (2010) offer a useful starting point: push
factors at home matter, but they are not the whole story.

**Reference**

Agbola, F. W., & Acupan, A. B. (2010). An empirical analysis of
international labour migration in the Philippines. *Economic Systems,
34*(4), 386–396. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecosys.2010.03.002
